# Successful developers: all, both, each, every, neither, none of, and no

[[PDFs/Grammar/all-both-each-every-neither-none-no--7956032454d1.pdf#page=1|Source PDF]] | [[Obsidian English/Imported/all-both-each-every-neither-none-no--7956032454d1/Exercises|Exercises]]

## Usage

The lesson combines quantifiers with expressions using **get**, in the context of successful developers (PDF p. 1). The article presents three traits: vision, persistence, and passion. The explanations below paraphrase the lesson; additional usage guidance is identified explicitly.

### All and both

- **All + plural noun** refers to every member of a group: *all developers*. Use a plural verb.
- **All + uncountable noun** refers to the whole amount: *all the work*. Use a singular verb.
- **Both + plural noun** refers to exactly two people or things: *both projects*. Use a plural verb.
- Before a determiner such as *the, my,* or *these*, use **all (of)** or **both (of)**: *all (of) my teammates; both (of) these developers*.
- Before an object pronoun, include **of**: *all of them; both of us*.

Additional guidance: **of** is optional before a determiner with *all* and *both*. The source's summary emphasizes forms with *of*, but Exercise 6 also correctly uses *all my close teammates* (PDF p. 1).

### Each and every

Both normally take **a singular countable noun and a singular verb**:

- **Each** emphasizes members individually.
- **Every** emphasizes all members of the group.

Additional guidance: *each* can refer to two or more members; *every* normally refers to three or more. Use **each of + determiner + plural noun**, or **each of + object pronoun**. Do not use *every of*: use **every one of** instead.

Compare *Each developer has a role* with *All developers have a role*. The overall meaning is similar, but the grammatical number changes.

### Neither, none of, and no

- **Neither + singular noun** means not one of two: *neither product*.
- **Neither of + determiner + plural noun**, or **neither of + object pronoun**, also refers to two: *neither of their projects; neither of them*.
- **None of + determiner + noun**, or **none of + object pronoun**, means not any of a specified group or amount: *none of these developers; none of them*.
- **No + noun** means not any. It can precede a singular countable noun, a plural countable noun, or an uncountable noun: *no developer; no bugs; no success*.

The source contrasts *both/neither* for two with *all/none of* for larger groups (PDF p. 1). Additional guidance: *all* and *none* are not restricted to groups larger than two, although *both* and *neither* make a two-member reference explicit.

Additional agreement guidance: use a singular verb with *neither + singular noun*. After *neither of + plural noun*, singular agreement is the conservative formal choice, while plural agreement is also common in informal usage. With *none of + plural noun*, singular or plural agreement is possible depending on emphasis and context. With an uncountable noun, use singular agreement: *None of the work is finished*.

### Common mistakes

- Use *every developer wants*, not *every developers want*.
- Use *both developers*, not *both developer*.
- Use *neither product*, but *neither of their products*.
- Use *no success*, not *none success*.
- Use *none of them*, not *no them*.
- Use *each of them* or *both of them*, not *each them*.
- *No* and *none* already express negation: *None of them gave up*.

### Expressions with get

These meanings paraphrase the article and its matching exercise (PDF p. 1).

| Expression | Meaning and pattern |
| --- | --- |
| get anywhere | Make progress or achieve something; often used in negatives and questions. |
| get to the top | Reach the highest level in a career or field; retain **to the**. |
| get under way | Begin to happen; **get something under way** means cause it to begin. |
| get off the ground | Start successfully or become established, especially a project or business. |
| get off to a good start | Have a successful beginning; retain **off to a**. |
| get ahead | Make progress or become more successful, especially in a career. |
| get in someone's way | Obstruct someone's progress; change the possessive to fit the person. |
| get on with something | Continue doing something; **with** can introduce a noun or an **-ing** form. |
| get + adjective | Become: *get frustrated; get difficult*. |
| get to be + noun phrase | Come to be or become over time. |

Additional form guidance: inflect *get* according to the sentence: *gets* in the third-person singular present and *got* in the simple past. After *will, won't,* and *don't*, use the base form *get*. After *let + object*, also use the base form: *Don't let setbacks get in your way*.

The article uses *got to be* to describe how Rails became influential, but Exercise 3 requires **gets + adjective** in *It gets frustrating*. The complete expression *get to be* does not fit that blank.
