# Rhetorical Questions

Source: [[Sources and Reading Notes#S5 - Why do we trust it so much|S5]], p. 1, sections 2-4.

A rhetorical question makes a point rather than asking for information. Its **form is a question**, but its **function is often a statement, criticism, suggestion, or expression of doubt**.

## Understand the intended point

Source example: “Can all those suggestions really be correct?”

The likely intended point is: **Some of those suggestions are probably incorrect.** The speaker does not expect someone to evaluate every suggestion immediately.

New examples:

| Rhetorical question | Likely intended point |
| --- | --- |
| Can one test really prove everything works? | One test probably cannot prove that. |
| Isn't that a risky way to deploy? | I think that is risky. |
| Who has time to repeat all this manually? | Repeating it manually takes too much time. |
| Why don't we check the assumptions first? | We should check the assumptions first. |

The last example may also be a genuine suggestion inviting a reply. Context and tone determine the function.

## Grammar patterns

### Modal question

`modal + subject + base verb?`

**Can a tool catch every bug?**

The modal goes before the subject. No **do** is needed. Related: [[Modal Verbs]].

### Do/does question

`(question word) + do/does + subject + base verb?`

**Why do people trust every result?**

**Does the reviewer really understand it?**

Keep the main verb in its base form: **Does she believe...?**, not *Does she believes...?*

### Negative be question

`isn't/aren't + subject + complement?`

**Isn't this approach careless?**

The speaker often expects agreement. In a genuine information question, it may simply check an assumption.

### Subject question

`who + verb + rest of question?`

**Who has time to audit every output?**

Here **who is the subject**, so there is no added *do*. Compare **Who do you trust?**, where *you* is the subject and *who* is the object.

## Tone and alternatives

Rhetorical questions can sound critical or impatient. For a neutral project discussion, you can turn the same idea into a statement or request:

- **Isn't that reckless?** → **I'm concerned about that risk.**
- **Why don't people test this?** → **Could we add a test before merging?**

Related: [[Presenting a Project Update]] for audience questions. “Does anyone have any questions?” usually invites an actual answer, so it normally is **not rhetorical**.
